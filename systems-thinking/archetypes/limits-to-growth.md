## Signature

A reinforcing engine (no minus signs) and a balancing loop that strengthens as the result approaches the carrying capacity (one minus). Early on the reinforcing loop dominates; as the constraint binds, dominance shifts and growth bends. The carrying capacity enters from outside the loops: a higher capacity weakens the slowing effect. Growth can end in decline by two different routes. If the limit's feedback is delayed (a common variant, not part of the archetype itself), growth runs past the limit before the brake bites, then falls back: overshoot. If the constraint itself erodes under the load, the ceiling drops and takes the result down with it.

## Symptoms

- Growth that felt exponential visibly bends, quarter after quarter.
- Effort on the growth engine yields less and less.
- People hunt for "what broke."

## Interventions

- The leverage is in the limit, not the growth engine. Remove or relax the limit if it can be removed; plan around it if it can't.
- Ask what, specifically, the constraint is, and whether it's truly fixed or something the system could grow past with real investment (if so, see Growth and Underinvestment).
- Watch leading indicators of the limit, especially when its feedback is delayed.

## Misapplications

- "Growth is slowing, we must have broken something": hunting for a component to repair, usually with a quick fix.
- Pushing harder on the growth engine (bigger recruiting drives, bigger bonuses). Against a binding limit, that mostly makes the balancing loop push back harder.
- Confusing the archetype with the 1972 *Limits to Growth* study, which was one contested, planetary application of the shape.

## Distinguish from

- **Growth and Underinvestment**: the special case where the limit is the system's own capacity, removable by investing.
- **Attractiveness Principle**: several limits at once, more than can be relieved.
- **Tragedy of the Commons**: the limit is a resource shared by several actors.
- **Escalation**: no wall at all, only an unbounded comparison.
