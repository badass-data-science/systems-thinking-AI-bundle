Pronounced kuh-NEV-in; a Welsh word usually translated as "habitat."

- **Clear** (called *simple* in the 2007 article; renamed later): cause and effect obvious to everyone; apply known best practice. *Example: renewing a known-good insurance policy.*
- **Complicated**: cause and effect exist and are discoverable with expertise or analysis; there's a knowable right answer. *Example: choosing the order of a seasonal ARIMA (SARIMA) forecasting model for a revenue series.*
- **Complex**: cause and effect are coherent only in retrospect; no upfront analysis reveals the pattern. Use small, safe-to-fail probes: probe, sense, respond. *Example: the first months of a competitor's price war, before its pattern is legible.*
- **Chaotic**: no stable cause and effect is perceivable; act immediately to stabilize, understand later. *Example: an active security incident in progress.*
- **Disorder** (center): not yet knowing which domain you're in.

Standard layout: complex top left, complicated top right, chaotic bottom left, clear bottom right.

The *complex* and *chaotic* labels borrow intuitions from complexity science and chaos theory, not their toolkits: calling a decision complex requires noticing that upfront analysis won't reveal the pattern, not running a simulation.

**The costliest misclassification** is treating a complex problem as complicated: expert analysis and a confident upfront plan where the domain calls for reversible probes.

**Fluency is not evidence of domain.** The domain is a property of the problem's causal structure; nothing about how persuasively an answer is phrased changes it.
